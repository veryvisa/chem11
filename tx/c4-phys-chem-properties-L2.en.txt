This is the brief on physical versus chemical properties. Matter has mass and takes up space, unlike heat. Properties show what substances are and become. First, physical properties—color, density, melting point, hardness, luster, viscosity, state—are observed without changing the substance. Chemical properties mean reacting to form new substances. Test. Can I measure this and keep the same stuff? Yes, physical. Rusts, burns, flammable, reacts, tarnishes, chemical.

Second, question. Classify each as physical or chemical. A. Ethanol boils at 78 degrees Celsius. B. Gasoline is flammable. C. Copper has a reddish-brown color. Steps. A. Boiling point is measured—still ethanol, just a gas—physical. B. Flammable means it can burn and become new substances—carbon dioxide, water, chemical. C. Color is seen by looking, physical. Answer. A. Physical. B. Chemical. C. Physical. Question. A student writes, sodium is a soft, shiny metal that reacts

violently with water. Steps. Soft. Hardness. Easily scratched. Physical. Shiny. Luster. Physical. Reacts violently with water. New substances form. Chemical. Answer. Soft. Shiny. Physical. Reacts with water. Chemical. Finally, question. Iron rust when exposed to oxygen and silver tarnishes in air. A friend says they are things that happen, not properties. Steps. Rust is a new substance made from iron and oxygen. Tarnish is a new dark substance on the silver surface. A property that describes how

a substance reacts to form a new substance is a chemical property. Answer. Both are chemical properties. Each describes reacting to form a new substance. Hardest question. A property is an ability, not an event. Would testing this sitting sample make a new substance? Yes. Chemical. No. Physical. For quick lists, underline the verb. Is, has, boils at, is hard, indicates physical. Rusts, burns, reacts, tarnishes, flammable, indicates chemical. Answer with a full word.
