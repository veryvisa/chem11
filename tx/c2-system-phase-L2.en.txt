This is the brief on system versus phase. So this is a quick rundown of chemistry counting rules for systems and phases to help secure quick marks. First, a system is exactly the part being studied. A phase is a uniform part. Different states or visible layers equal different phases.

The rules. Ice plus water is two. Oil plus water is two. Salt water is one because it looks the same everywhere. Mixed gases are always one phase.

And a visible boundary means more than one phase. Your class answer key marks if two substances look uniform throughout, they must be one phase as true. Many textbooks would add that looking uniform is a good sign but not proof. On this test, answer true. Second, let's hit some worked examples.

One, a student measures temp changes when ice melts and lemonade. The systems, ice cubes and lemonade. Another scientist records gas pressure in a sealed juice bottle. The system is the sealed bottle, juice and gas. Example two, how many phases?

A beaker holds water, an oil layer, ice cubes and sand. Water is one, oil is two, ice is three, and sand makes four. Answer is four. Example three, a system can have more than one phase. True, ice and water is two.

But a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen gas forms two phases. False. Gases mix completely into one. Finally, the hardest questions use this strict addition rule. All gases together equal one phase.

Each liquid layer equals one phase. Each different solid equals one phase. Just add them up. Exam tip. For phase counts, list every visibly different part in a column and number them.

For identify the system, copy all the things being studied. The class answers name both parts. E.g. sugar and hot tea.
