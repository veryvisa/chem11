This is the brief on system versus phase, key terms and common mistakes. Consider this a quick, essential review to help you navigate tricky definitions and common test traps regarding chemical systems and phases. First, the key terms. You might ask, system? Well, that's just the part of the universe you're studying, what you focus on. Phase? That's a part of a system that is uniform in composition and properties.

So, phases in oil and water? Two. In salt water? One. A mixture of two gases? How many phases? Always one. What does a visible boundary tell you?

It means there is more than one phase. Second, common mistakes and the fix. Mistake one is counting substances instead of phases. The fix? Salt water has two substances but only one phase. Mistake two is saying two gases make two phases.

The fix? Gases always mix into one phase. Mistake three is naming only a part of the system. The fix for sugar dissolving in hot tea? The system is the sugar and the hot tea. Finally, traps on the test.

Trap one is thinking two gases equal two phases. Remember, gases always mix into one phase. Trap two is thinking salt water equals two phases because it has two substances. Nope, it is one phase. Your class answer key marks if two substances look uniform throughout, they must be one phase as true.

Many textbooks would add that looking uniform is a good sign but not proof. On this test, answer true.
