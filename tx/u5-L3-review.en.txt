This is the brief on Unit 5 review. Phase changes and energy. This review rapidly breaks down phase transitions, how they map onto heating curves, and the exact kinetic energy rules needed to ace the test. First, phase changes are literally always physical. Solid to gas directly, that's sublimation, starting as a solid, like dry ice. Deposition starts as a gas, making frost. Gas to liquid is condensation, making drops. Liquid to gas is evaporation, making gas. Remember, melting,

evaporation, and sublimation absorb energy. Crucially, textbooks call liquid to gas vaporization, but your class handout expects evaporation. Use that, reserving boiling for the flat step at 100 degrees Celsius. Second, heating curves. Diagonal lines mean temperature is changing and one phase is warming. Horizontal lines, a phase change is happening, two phases are actually present, and temperature stays totally constant. Right, temperature doesn't keep rising while ice melts,

so why does it flatline during boiling? Because heat breaks attractions to separate particles, boosting translational kinetic energy instead of making them move faster. On cooling curves, flat parts are condensation at 100 degrees and freezing at zero. Also, class notes put heat added on the x-axis, whereas textbooks use time, but the shape's exactly the same. Finally, kinetic energy, the energy of motion. Higher temperature means particles move faster. Rotational energy is just

spinning. Vibrational means bonds stretch and bend right in place. Translational means the whole molecule physically moves from place to place. Don't mix them up. And remember, translational is most important for phase changes. On this test, solid equals mostly vibrating in place. If asked what increases while a solid warms, give the class answer. Vibrational plus some translational.
