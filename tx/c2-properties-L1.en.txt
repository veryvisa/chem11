This is the brief on BC Chemistry 11, Key Terms and Common Mistakes. We're doing a rapid-fire review of essential chemistry definitions and specific traps designed to trip you up on exams, ensuring clarity on exactly what to write. First, let's establish the core facts before seeing how they get confused. Malleability means being hammered or rolled into sheets without breaking.

Ductility is being drawn into a thin wire without breaking. Hardness? That's strictly the resistance to being scratched. Now, high viscosity flows slowly like honey, while low viscosity flows easily like water. For vapor, the class version is a gas from evaporation above room temperature, 25 degrees Celsius, and the vapor pressure unit is KPA. Finally, diffusion goes from high to low concentration until equilibrium is reached, meaning it's evenly spread.

Second, knowing the terms is only half the battle. The rest is avoiding test traps when things get mixed up. Malleable versus ductile? Don't mistakenly think malleable means wire. The fix?

Malleable is for sheets. Just think mallet equals hammer. And ductile is for wires. With viscosity, don't think high viscosity is runny. No.

High viscosity is thick and slow. Low viscosity is runny. For diffusion, a common error is writing low to high concentration. It always goes high to low. Lastly, don't call luster color.

Luster is about how light reflects. Shiny versus dull, not which color it is. To summarize, the class notes define vapor as gas formed by evaporation above room temperature, 25 degrees Celsius. Most textbooks define vapor as the gas of a substance that is normally a liquid or solid at room temperature. And note that evaporation happens at any temperature.

On this test, use the class version.
