This is the brief on Unit 4 Review, Properties and Changes. Matter has mass and takes up space, so energy isn't matter. We're breaking down exact definitions, examples, and common mistakes regarding properties and changes. First, physical properties are observed without changing the substance. Chemical properties describe how it reacts to form new substances.

Think rusts, burns, flammable, reacts with, or tarnishes. Mercury is a liquid at room temperature. Physical or chemical? Physical because it describes its state. Remember, color is physical since you just look at it.

Only changes color by reacting is chemical. Also, flammable is just the ability to react, even if nothing's burning currently. Second, extensive and intensive are both physical properties. Extensive depends on amount, mass, volume, length. Intensive doesn't.

Density, melting point, boiling point, color. The fastest test? Cut the sample in half. Value halves? Extensive. Stays the same? Intensive. Temperature, extensive or intensive?

Intensive. Don't call it extensive assuming a bigger pot has more heat. It's the same in a cup or bathtub at 40 degrees. Also, don't call density extensive because a big block is heavier. Heavier is mass.

Density stays the same. Finally, changes. Chemical changes form hard-to-reverse new substances, showing clues like new color, gas, precipitate, heat, or light. Physical changes alter form, phase, or appearance. No new substance forms.

Face changes? Physical. All six. An egg is fried. Physical or chemical?

Chemical. You can't unfry it, meaning new substance is formed. Don't call boiling chemical because a gas is produced. Ask what the gas is. It's the exact same substance, water vapor, making it physical.

To summarize, with one last mistake to avoid. Calling breaking glass or shredding paper chemical because you cannot undo it. Hard to reverse is only a clue. No new substance. Physical.
