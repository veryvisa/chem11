This is the brief on Unit 2 Reveal, Physical Properties of Matter. We're walking through core definitions, common mistakes, and specific class handout rules. First, matter is anything with mass taking up space, or volume. We've got four states, solid, liquid, gas, and plasma. So which state is compressible? Gas only. Its particles are far apart. And a liquid's shape and volume? Indefinite shape, taking the container's shape, but definite volume. Particles stay in close contact, but slide. Don't make this mistake. Volume doesn't change. 250 milliliters stays 250. Light and heat are energy, not matter. Textbooks list three everyday states calling liquids nearly incompressible. On this test, four states only gases are compressible. Second, physical properties. Malleability? Hammered or rolled into sheets without breaking, like aluminum foil. Ductility? Drawn into thin wires without breaking, like copper. Water isn't ductile. Hardness? Resistance to scratching. Diamonds can't be scratched. Viscosity? Honey versus water? Honey is high, flowing slowly. Water's low, flowing easily. Class defines vapor as gas from evaporation above room temperature, 25 degrees Celsius, measured in kilopascals. Textbooks differ, but use the class version. Diffusion moves from high to low concentration, until evenly spread into equilibrium. A system is the part of the universe you're studying. A phase is uniform in composition and properties. Phase? Phases in oil and water? Two. In salt water? One. A mixture of two gases? How many phases? Always one. Visible boundary? More than one phase. Don't count substances instead of phases. To summarize systems and phases using the class handout's version, your class answer key marks, if two substances look uniform throughout, they must be one phase as true. And while many textbooks would add that looking uniform is a good sign but not proof, on this test, answer true.
